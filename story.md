# The Emperor's Watchmaker

The year was 1814. Paris shivered under a gray April sky, and the Allies were closing in.

Louis Breguet wiped his hands on a cloth and looked at the pocket watch on his bench. It was a Breguet No. 160—the "Marie Antoinette"—though the Queen had lost her head thirty years before it was finished. Perpetual calendar. Equation of time. Thermometer. A masterpiece of 823 parts, forty-four years in the making.

And now, the Emperor wanted it modified.

"Three days," the aide-de-camp had said, his boots still muddy from the road. "The Emperor departs for Fontainebleau. He wants the watch to show him something other than hours."

Breguet had asked what.

The young officer had leaned close. "He wants to know how much time France has left."

---

Napoleon arrived at midnight. No entourage. No torchlight. Just a silhouette in the famous gray coat, the bicorne pulled low, one hand tucked into his waistcoat.

"Citizen Breguet." The voice was a rasp, worn thin by Russia, by Leipzig, by the thousand cuts of 1813.

"Sire." Breguet bowed, his bad knee protesting. "The watch is ready."

Napoleon crossed the workshop in three strides. His fingers—short, strong, the fingers that had traced maps across Europe—closed around the watch. He didn't wind it. Didn't open it. Just held it.

"Show me."

Breguet gestured to the second watch on the bench. Identical in appearance. "The modification is in the repeater mechanism, Sire. When activated, it doesn't chime the hour. It counts down."

"Counts down to what?"

"To whatever you program it for, Sire. A date. A battle. An anniversary." Breguet swallowed. "I set it for Paris. The Treaty of Chaumont expires in sixty days. After that, the Allies have pledged not to negotiate."

Napoleon's eyes—gray, intensity compressed into laser focus—met the watchmaker's. "And if Paris falls before sixty days?"

"Then the watch stops, Sire. It only counts down while the mainspring has power. Like France." Breguet's voice steadied. "Like any empire. The energy runs out."

A long silence. The workshop clock ticked. Somewhere, a dog barked.

Napoleon opened the watch. The dial glowed in the lamplight: *60:00:00:00*. Sixty days. Zero hours. Zero minutes. Zero seconds.

He pressed the repeater slide.

A chime. Pure, silver. *One.*

"Sixty days," Napoleon murmured. "To save an empire. To save a dynasty." He snapped the watch shut. "My son is three years old. The King of Rome. He should have sixty years. Six hundred."

"Sire—"

"The watchmaker of the Queen makes watches for the Emperor." A ghost of a smile, gone as quickly. "History's irony, Citizen Breguet. The Marie Antoinette for the Corsican ogre."

"It measures time, Sire. Time belongs to no one."

"Doesn't it?" Napoleon turned the watch in his hands. "I've spent twenty years trying to bend time to my will. Marched armies across calendars. Turned years into victories, victories into years." He looked up. "Time is the one enemy I've never defeated."

He tucked the watch into his waistcoat pocket—*his* pocket, the one over his heart.

"Three days ago, I had 600,000 men. Today, perhaps 70,000. In sixty days..." He shrugged. "The arithmetic is simple."

"Sire, the watch—"

"Is perfect. As always." Napoleon moved toward the door, then paused. "Citizen Breguet. If the watch stops... if the mainspring breaks... can it be repaired?"

"Any watch can be repaired, Sire. Given time. Given parts. Given a steady hand."

"Ah." Napoleon's hand rested on the latch. "But the parts must exist. And the hand must be steady. And there must be *time*."

He left into the night.

Breguet stood alone in his workshop, listening to the clock tick. *Tick. Tick. Tick.*

Sixty days.

---

The news came in fragments. Couriers. Rumors. The Moniteur's increasingly desperate bulletins.

*Montmirail. Château-Thierry. Vauchamps.* Victories. Brilliant, desperate, *useless* victories. Each one bought days, not weeks. The Allies simply retreated, regrouped, advanced again. Napoleon moved like a stroke of lightning—here, there, everywhere at once—but lightning doesn't last.

Breguet watched the second watch on his bench. *42:18:03:12.*

Forty-two days.

He began to understand what he had built. Not a countdown. A *witness.*

Every tick was a choice. Every hour a battle. Every day a decision: fight or negotiate, advance or retreat, the Emperor or the father.

The watch didn't measure time. It measured *will.*

---

March 30. The Allies entered Paris.

Breguet stood at his window, watching the Prussian uniforms in the Place Vendôme. The second watch read *00:00:00:00.*

It had stopped at midnight.

He waited. Hours. Days.

On April 6, a message arrived. Not from the Emperor. From the new government.

*Citizen Breguet. The Emperor has abdicated. He departs for Elba. You are summoned to Fontainebleau.*

---

The palace was quiet. The grandeur felt hollow, like a gutted clock case.

Napoleon waited in the small study where he'd signed the abdication. He looked older. The gray coat hung loose. But his eyes—

He held out his hand. The watch lay in his palm. The glass was cracked. The case dented. But the movement—

*It was still running.*

*00:00:00:00.* Frozen at zero. But the balance wheel swung. *Tick. Tick. Tick.*

"How?" Breguet breathed.

"I forgot to wind it," Napoleon said. "For three days after Paris fell. I simply... forgot." He smiled, thin and tired. "But the mainspring had reserve. Forty hours of reserve. Just like France, eh? Always a little more fight in the spring than you calculated."

He pressed the repeater.

Silence. The chime mechanism was jammed—dented from a fall, or a blow, or the frantic pocket of a man riding through the night.

"Can you fix it?"

Breguet took the watch. His fingers found the damage instantly. A bent lever. A cracked jewel. Repairable. *Always repairable.*

"Yes, Sire. In a few hours."

"Good." Napoleon sank into a chair. "Do it here. I want to hear it chime. One more time."

Breguet worked at a side table, tools spread on velvet. The Emperor sat behind him, silent. Watching. Waiting.

The repair was delicate. Microscopic. Breguet's hands didn't tremble—watchmakers' hands never tremble—but his breath came shallow.

*There.* The lever seated. The jewel aligned.

He wound the repeater spring. Pressed the slide.

*Ding. Dong.*

One chime. For the first day of exile.

Napoleon closed his eyes. "Again."

Breguet pressed the slide.

*Ding. Dong. Ding. Dong.*

Two chimes. For the Emperor and the King of Rome.

"Again."

*Ding. Dong. Ding. Dong. Ding. Dong.*

Three. For France. For the Army. For the Code.

"Again."

Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.

Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen.

The chimes filled the room. Silver notes in stale air.

Twenty. Thirty. Forty. Fifty.

*Sixty.*

Sixty chimes. For sixty days. For the arithmetic of empire.

The last note faded. Napoleon opened his eyes.

"Citizen Breguet."

"Sire."

"The watch doesn't stop at zero, does it?"

Breguet looked at the dial. *00:00:00:00.* But the balance wheel swung. *Tick. Tick. Tick.*

"No, Sire. The countdown reaches zero. But the movement continues. It becomes a stopwatch. Counting *up* from nothing."

"From nothing." Napoleon repeated it slowly. "From zero. A new calendar."

He took the watch back. Snapped it shut. Returned it to his waistcoat pocket—over his heart.

"Thank you, Citizen Breguet. For the watch. For the forty hours of reserve. For..." He gestured vaguely. "For understanding that time isn't what we lose. It's what we make of what remains."

---

At the coast, Napoleon boarded the *Undaunted*. The British frigate waited, patient as tide.

Breguet stood on the quay, watching the small figure in the gray coat pace the quarterdeck. The Emperor of Elba. Sovereign of twelve square leagues. Ruler of 12,000 souls.

He would last three hundred days.

Breguet knew this because he had built the second watch—the one that stayed in Paris. He had set it for three hundred days when Napoleon left Fontainebleau.

*300:00:00:00.*

It sat on his bench now, ticking down. *299:23:59:45. 299:23:59:44.*

Three hundred days until the Eagle flew again.

Three hundred days until Waterloo.

Three hundred days until the final zero—and the *next* countdown up from nothing.

Because empires fell. Emperors died. Watchmakers grew old and blind.

But time...

Time kept ticking.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

And somewhere, a mainspring always had a little more reserve than you calculated.

*Tempus fugit, sed imperium manet.*

Time flies, but the empire remains.

*In the mechanism. In the memory. In the next winding.*

Breguet turned toward his shop. He had work to do. A repeater to perfect. A tourbillon to patent. A Queen's watch to finally deliver, thirty-four years late.

He had time.

*He had nothing but time.*
